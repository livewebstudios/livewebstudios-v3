A booker replied in March. "Check back with me in the fall, we'll have room."

It is now December and nobody checked back.

That gig was yours. It did not go to a better band, it went to whoever was in the inbox in September. The follow-up did not fail because anybody was lazy. It failed because the only place that conversation existed was one person's memory and a buried email thread, and neither of those survives a busy summer.

## The Follow-Up You Forgot

Most bands lose more work to disorganization than to rejection.

Think about how a booking conversation actually lives. A message on Instagram from one member, an email from another, a phone call somebody took in a parking lot, a "sure, talk in the spring" that everybody heard and nobody wrote down.

Six weeks later, nobody is certain whether that room was pitched, answered, or just imagined. So nobody follows up, because following up on something you already followed up on feels worse than doing nothing.

A pipeline fixes exactly this and nothing else. It is not a system for being organized. It is a system for never wondering.

## Five Columns Is Enough

Open a spreadsheet. Google Sheets is free and everybody in the band can see it, which matters more than any feature.

Five columns.

<ul>
  <li><strong>Venue.</strong> The room, and the town.</li>
  <li><strong>Contact.</strong> The person's name and how you reach them.</li>
  <li><strong>Last touch.</strong> The date you last spoke, and one line about what was said.</li>
  <li><strong>Next step.</strong> What you do next, in plain words. "Email Dana about February."</li>
  <li><strong>Next-step date.</strong> When you do it.</li>
</ul>

That is the whole tool. Resist adding more columns for a few months, because every extra field is one more reason not to update the row, and a pipeline nobody updates is worse than no pipeline.

One person owns the sheet. Everybody can read it, one person writes to it. Otherwise you get three versions of the same venue and nobody trusts any of them.

## The Stages

Add a sixth column if you want, for stage, and keep it to six words.

Researching. Pitched. Followed up. Talking. Booked. Rebook.

A room moves down that list and occasionally back up one. Researching is a place you found and have not contacted. Pitched means the email went out. Followed up means you sent the one polite nudge and heard nothing. Talking means an actual conversation is happening. Booked is obvious. Rebook is where a room goes after you have played it, with a next-step date a few months out.

That last stage is the one that pays. Every room you have ever played should be sitting in Rebook with a date on it, which turns [the follow-up that gets you rebooked](follow-up-after-a-gig-to-get-rebooked.html) from a thing you remember to do into a thing the sheet reminds you about.

Colour the stages if it helps you see the shape at a glance. Mine is ugly and I do not care.

## A Ten-Minute Weekly Review

Same day every week. Sunday morning with coffee works for a lot of people because the gig is fresh and the week has not started.

Open the sheet. Sort by next-step date. Anything due this week or overdue, do it now or move the date with a reason.

That is the entire review. Ten minutes.

What makes it work is that you are not deciding what to do. You decided that last time, when the conversation was fresh and you knew exactly what came next. Sunday you is just executing, which is a much easier job than Sunday you trying to remember what March you was thinking.

I run my web business on exactly this, with the same five columns and the same weekly ten minutes. It is not sophisticated. It just means nothing falls through, and over a couple of years "nothing falls through" turns out to be most of the advantage.

## Let AI Help With Reminders

Once the sheet exists, AI becomes genuinely useful, because now it has something real to work from.

Paste a row into a chat and ask for the follow-up.

> Here's a row from my booking sheet: venue, contact name, last contact date, and what they said. Write a short, friendly follow-up email for this week. Two or three sentences. Don't oversell, and don't invent anything that isn't in the row.

Thirty seconds and you have a draft to edit. Do a month's worth in one sitting and the weekly review turns into pressing send.

It can also read the whole sheet and tell you things. Which rooms have gone cold. Which towns you have never pitched. Which contacts you have spoken to three times without booking, which usually means it is time to stop.

The rule is the same as always: it drafts, you check. [AI is fast at the tedious part and confidently wrong about the specific part](use-ai-to-research-venues-for-gigs.html), and a booking sheet is nothing but specifics.

Start it today with the ten rooms you already know. Ten minutes to build, ten minutes a week to run, and you will never again find out in December that somebody told you to check back in the fall.
