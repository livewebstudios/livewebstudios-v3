Two people are sitting on a couch with a phone between them, eight months out from a wedding. They type "wedding band near me" and look at whatever comes back.

Your band is thirty minutes away and perfect for them. You do not appear.

That is local search, and it is the one kind of marketing where somebody is actively trying to hire a band and cannot find you. I do this for a living for other people's businesses, and the good news is that most of what moves the needle for a band is not technical at all. It is mostly about being findable and being specific about where you work.

## How "Near Me" Searches Work

Search engines answer local questions differently than general ones. When somebody types "near me," or names a town, the results reshuffle around where that person is standing.

Three things mostly decide who appears. Whether the search engine believes you exist as a real, current business. Whether it can tell what area you serve. And whether other people seem to think you are any good.

Notice what is not on that list: how good your band is. Nothing about a search result knows that, any more than [the first thirty seconds of a booker's attention](what-venue-bookers-look-at-first.html) does. This is entirely a findability problem, and findability is fixable in an afternoon.

## A Google Business Profile for Your Band

This is the single highest-value hour you can spend, and most bands have never done it.

A Google Business Profile is the panel that appears on the right side of a search, with photos, reviews, and a contact button. It is free. Bands qualify, and you set it up as a service-area business rather than one with a storefront, because nobody is driving to your house.

<ul>
  <li>Pick the categories that match what you do: live music, entertainment, wedding service.</li>
  <li>Set your service area to the towns and counties you will actually drive to.</li>
  <li>Add real photos from real gigs. Not logos. Rooms with people in them.</li>
  <li>Link it to your own website, not a social profile.</li>
  <li>Put a phone number and an email on it that somebody checks.</li>
</ul>

Then keep your name, phone number, and email identical everywhere they appear. Your site, your profile, your socials, any directory you are listed in. Search engines cross-check that information, and three different phone numbers across three places makes you look like three half-abandoned businesses instead of one working band.

## Name the Towns You Play

Here is the part bands leave out, and it costs them the most.

If your website never mentions a single town, a search engine has nothing to connect you to a place. You cannot show up for "band in [town]" if the word [town] appears nowhere on your site.

So say it. Plainly, in ordinary sentences, in the places it belongs.

Your homepage can say where you are based and roughly how far you travel. Your gig calendar does this work automatically if every listing includes the town. A short line on your contact page listing the counties or regions you cover is worth more than it looks.

What you do not do is paste a list of ninety town names at the bottom of a page. That was a trick fifteen years ago and now it mostly just reads as desperate to the humans and gets ignored by the machines.

Real mentions, in real sentences, on pages that are actually about something. That is the whole technique.

## Reviews Move the Needle

Reviews do two jobs at once. They help you appear, and they help you get chosen once you appear.

A couple comparing three bands with no reviews and one band with eleven reviews is not really comparing four bands.

The problem is that nobody leaves a review unprompted. You have to ask, and the window is small. Ask the day after, while they are still glowing about the night, and make it a single tap.

> Thanks again for having us on Saturday, it was a genuinely fun room. If you have two minutes, a quick review here would mean a lot to us: [direct link]

Send that to the couple, the host, the event coordinator, and the venue manager. Four asks, and one of them usually lands. Do it after every private event and you will build something in a year that no amount of advertising buys.

## Pages That Match What People Search

The last piece is having a page that answers the question somebody typed.

If couples search "wedding band," you need a page about weddings. Not a paragraph buried on your homepage. An actual page that talks about how a reception night works, what the sets look like, what you need from the venue, and how to check your availability.

Same for corporate and private events, if you do them. Same for any style of act you get hired as.

A booker or a planner who lands on a page that speaks directly to their situation feels understood, and a search engine reading that page finally has something clear to match against. It works on both audiences at once, which is rare.

None of this happens overnight and anybody promising you a specific ranking is guessing. What I can tell you from years of doing this for small businesses is that the bands who show up are almost never the ones who did something clever. They are the ones who claimed their profile, said where they play, and asked for reviews. That is it. It works because so few competitors bother, and it works from the same foundation as everything else here: [one website you own](why-a-facebook-page-isnt-a-band-website.html), with your name on it.
