You send the monthly newsletter. Dates up top, a story about the second set, a photo of somebody's birthday party storming the dance floor.

Two of the people who open it book talent for a living.

They do not care about the birthday party. They care whether you are any good and whether you are free in April, and the email they just read answered neither question. Meanwhile the fan three names down the list would have been bored senseless by an availability update. Same list, two completely different jobs, and one of them is always getting served badly.

## Different People, Different Reasons

A fan signed up because they had a good night and want another one.

A booker, a planner, or a venue manager signed up because you are a vendor they might use. That is a working relationship, not an enthusiasm.

Those two people open email for different reasons, at different frequencies, in different moods. Trying to write one message that lands for both produces something that lands for neither, and the usual result is that you write to the fans and quietly let the professional side of your list go cold.

Which is a waste, because [the list is the one audience you own](why-bands-need-an-email-list.html), and the half of it that can actually hire you is the half most bands ignore.

## What Fans Want

Dates, first, always. Then something human.

Where you are playing, what happened at the last one, a photo they might be in, a song you added. Monthly is the right rhythm. Short is better than thorough.

The tone is the tone you would use talking to somebody at the merch table. Warm, a bit funny, not selling.

What fans do not want is your availability calendar, your press quotes, or anything that reads like a sales sheet. The moment a fan email starts sounding like a pitch, people stop opening it.

## What Bookers and Planners Want

Almost the opposite.

Send to this group a few times a year. Three or four emails, not twelve. Each one should be short enough to read standing up and should answer the questions they are paid to ask.

<ul>
  <li>Rooms you have played since you last wrote. Names they recognize.</li>
  <li>One new clip, linked, not attached.</li>
  <li>The months you have open.</li>
  <li>Anything that reduces their risk: a new stage plot, a testimonial, a type of event you have started doing.</li>
</ul>

No stories about the birthday party. No inside jokes. This person is deciding whether to spend their budget on you, and everything in the email should help them decide.

Tone is professional and brief. Think of it as a polite reminder that you exist and are working, which is genuinely all it needs to be.

## How Often to Send Each

Fans: monthly. Bookers: quarterly, or four times a year lined up with when calendars get filled in your area.

That timing matters more than the content. A booker email that lands the week they are building next season's schedule is worth ten that land in the middle of it. Ask a friendly venue manager when they book, and aim at that.

The nice thing about four emails a year is that it is genuinely easy to keep up. Two hours, four times a year. You will never have an excuse.

## Tags Beat Two Accounts

Do not open a second account. Use one, and tag people.

Every email tool worth using lets you label a contact and then send to just that label. Mailchimp calls them tags, other tools call them segments, and the idea is the same everywhere. One list, one place to manage unsubscribes, two audiences.

Tag on the way in. The QR code at the merch table feeds the fan tag. The form on your contact page can ask whether somebody is booking or coming to a show. Anybody you have actually worked with gets tagged as a booker by hand, which takes five seconds after each gig.

Two rules on the legal side, and I am a web guy rather than a lawyer, so confirm the specifics for where you are. <!-- VERIFY --> Only email people who gave you their address or who you have genuinely done business with. And every email, to either group, needs a working unsubscribe link. <!-- VERIFY -->

Scraping addresses off venue websites and blasting them is not a booker list. It is spam, it gets your domain flagged, and the people you most want to reach are exactly the people who will remember it.

The monthly email you are already writing is more than half the work here. [Keep that template](monthly-band-newsletter-template.html) for the fans, write a short, plain version four times a year for the professionals, and you are covering both audiences for about the same effort it takes to serve one badly.
