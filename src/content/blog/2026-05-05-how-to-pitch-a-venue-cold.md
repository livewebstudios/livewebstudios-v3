A booker opens their inbox on a Tuesday morning. Fourteen band emails since Friday. Eleven of them start with the same sentence about being an exciting new act with a sound that has to be heard.

They get deleted in a batch, unread past the first line.

Yours does not have to be one of those. A cold pitch that works is short, specific to that room, and easy to say yes to. It takes about ten minutes to write and most of that time goes into the part before the writing.

## Why Most Pitch Emails Get Deleted

Three reasons, over and over.

The first is that the email is about the band. Every sentence is a claim about how good you are, which a stranger has no reason to believe and no way to check quickly.

The second is that it clearly went to fifty venues. No mention of the room, no mention of a night, nothing that proves you have ever been inside the building.

The third is length. A booker is not reading four paragraphs from somebody they have never heard of. If they have to scroll, they are gone.

Underneath all of it sits one question the booker is actually asking: is this band going to be worth the risk and easy to deal with? [That is the same question they answer by looking at your site](what-venue-bookers-look-at-first.html), and your email is just the thing that gets them to go look.

## Know the Room Before You Write

Spend five minutes on the venue before you write a word.

Find out what nights they do live music and what kind of act plays each one. A room with a singer-songwriter on Wednesdays and a dance band on Saturdays is really two venues, and pitching the wrong one marks you instantly as somebody who did not look.

Find the person's name. A booking page, a Facebook post, an Instagram bio. Writing "Hi Dana" instead of "To whom it may concern" changes the temperature of the whole email.

And find one honest, specific thing you can say about the room. You played down the street. A band you know has a good night there. You have been in as a customer. One true sentence is enough, and it separates you from every mass email in the folder.

## The Five-Sentence Pitch

Keep it this short. Seriously.

> Hi Dana,
>
> I play in a four-piece cover band out of [town], and we've been doing Saturdays at [room they'd recognize] and [another one] for the past year or so. I noticed you run live music on Thursdays and I think we'd fit that slot well: we do a long dance set that keeps people at the bar rather than heading out at ten.
>
> Everything is here, including a live clip and our dates: [one link]
>
> Would you have anything open in February or March? Happy to work around whatever you've already got booked.
>
> Thanks,
> Jon

Look at what is in there. Where you are from, rooms you have played, the specific night you are pitching, why you fit that night, one link, and a real question with a real timeframe.

Look at what is not in there. No attachments. No genre adjectives. No claim that you are the best anything. No paragraph about how the band got together.

The one link goes to your press kit, and [what belongs on that page](what-belongs-in-a-band-epk.html) is the whole rest of the pitch. The email's only job is to get it opened.

## Subject Lines Bookers Actually Open

Short, plain, and about the room rather than about you.

<ul>
  <li>Thursdays at [venue name]: cover band from [town]</li>
  <li>[Band name], four-piece, available Feb and March</li>
  <li>Booking inquiry: [venue name], weekend dates</li>
</ul>

What gets ignored: anything in all caps, anything with more than one exclamation point, and anything that sounds like a press release. "AMAZING new band available NOW!!" reads as spam because it is written the way spam is written.

Sentence case. Name the venue or the night if you can fit it. A booker scanning a phone screen sees maybe forty characters, so front-load the useful part.

## Following Up Without Being a Pest

One follow-up. About a week later. Two sentences.

> Hi Dana, just floating this back up in case it got buried. Still have February and March open on our end if anything fits.

Then stop. If there is no answer after that, the answer is no for now, and it is almost never personal. Bookers are busy, calendars fill six months out, and plenty of rooms are simply not looking.

What you do instead of a third email is keep playing and check back in three or four months with something new to say. A new clip. A room you have added. A night that went well nearby. That is a fresh pitch, not a follow-up, and it lands completely differently.

The bands that fill calendars are not the ones with the best emails. They are the ones still politely showing up in the inbox two years later, with something a little better to show every time.
