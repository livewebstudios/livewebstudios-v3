A keyboard player comes down with something on a Thursday. The gig is Saturday night. The bandleader has about a day to fix it, and nobody is holding auditions.

So they text two people. Whoever answers first and checks out gets the night.

That is how most sub work gets handed out. No posting, no application. A name surfaces in somebody's head, that name gets looked up in about a minute, and the call goes out. I have been on both ends of that text. I have also spent twenty-plus years building websites for a living, which means I know what happens during that minute.

## How a Sub Call Really Happens

It starts with a recommendation. Almost always. A bandleader asks the guitarist, the guitarist says "call this drummer, she's great and she's easy," and that is the entire selection process.

Notice what did not happen. Nobody watched a full set. Nobody compared résumés.

What the recommendation buys you is a look. It does not close the deal by itself, because the bandleader still has to feel all right about handing a stranger their Saturday night.

## The Search You Never See

Here is the part most players never think about. After the recommendation, the bandleader looks you up.

Your name goes into a search bar. Instagram, Facebook, plain old Google, whichever is closest to their thumb. It takes about a minute and you will never know it happened.

If what comes back reads like a working musician, the text goes out. If what comes back is confusing, or a profile nobody has touched in years, or nothing at all, the bandleader quietly moves to the second name on the list.

That is not unfair. Sit in their chair for a second. They are about to trust somebody they have never met with a room full of paying customers on a night they cannot get back.

## What a Bandleader Needs to Find

It is a short list, and none of it is about impressing anybody.

<ul>
  <li>What you play, and which styles you actually cover.</li>
  <li>Who you have worked with. Acts and rooms, not adjectives.</li>
  <li>One clip where you are clearly playing live with other people.</li>
  <li>A way to reach you that works on a phone, today.</li>
</ul>

That is the whole thing. A guitarist who can show those four in thirty seconds gets more calls than a stronger guitarist nobody can size up. I wish that were not true. It is.

The fastest fix is a page with your own name on it. Not the band's page. Yours. Bands come and go, and your name stays the same for your whole working life. [What goes on a freelance player's site](website-for-freelance-musicians.html) is a longer conversation, and it is worth having.

## Staying on Other Bandleaders' Radar

The recommendation is the hard part, and you cannot buy one. You earn it by being around and being decent to work with.

Some of that is just showing up. Take the gig that feels a little beneath you now and then. Say yes to the last-minute Tuesday. The person who called you remembers, and so does everybody else on that stage.

The rest is staying visible in a quiet way. Post the occasional photo from a gig and tag the act you played with. Say something when a player you know has a good week. You are not building an audience here. You are staying in the mental rolodex of maybe fifteen bandleaders in your area, which is a small and winnable game.

And answer your messages. A player who replies within the hour gets called again. A player who takes three days gets called when nobody else picks up.

## Be the Easy Yes

Reliability is the reputation. Not your chops.

Here is what gets a sub rehired. They learned the material without being asked twice. They showed up early. They did not complain about the stage or the monitor mix. They packed out their own things and then helped somebody else with theirs.

None of that is talent. All of it is a decision you make in advance.

The players in my area who work the most are rarely the most gifted ones. They are the ones a bandleader can call at nine on a Thursday without any anxiety about how the night will go. Two names, one phone call, and that feeling decides it.

Venues run the same check before they answer an email, by the way. [What a booker looks at first](what-venue-bookers-look-at-first.html) is a longer version of the same question a bandleader asks: is this person going to make my night easier or harder?

Make yourself easy to say yes to. Then make it easy to find out that you are.
