It is Sunday. You got in at two, you have a real job tomorrow, and somewhere on your phone there are four things you meant to do about the band.

You will do none of them, and next Sunday will look the same.

That is not a discipline problem. It is a design problem. Marketing a band fails for working people because it has no shape, so it competes with sleep and always loses. Give it a shape and two hours a week, and it stops being a thing you feel guilty about and starts being a thing that quietly runs.

## A System Beats Motivation

Everything in this series comes down to one idea. Decide once, then execute on a schedule.

Motivation gets a band through the first three weeks. A system gets a band through a year, which is the timescale on which any of this actually works.

Two hours a week, split into two blocks. One hour on booking, one hour on everything that reaches an audience. Plus a short monthly look at numbers and a short routine after every gig.

That is the whole thing. It fits around a day job and a gig schedule because it has to, and because nothing that does not fit around those two things will survive contact with December.

Pick your days now and put them in your calendar like a rehearsal. Sunday morning and Wednesday evening works for a lot of people.

The other half of a system is that it removes the arguing. When everybody in the band knows the booking hour happens on Sunday and the posts get built on the first of the month, nobody has to be nagged and nobody has to volunteer. The work has a home.

## The Weekly Hour for Booking

This hour is about the calendar twelve months from now, not next weekend.

Start with the sheet. Open [your booking pipeline](simple-booking-pipeline-for-bands.html), sort by next-step date, and do whatever is due. That is usually two or three short emails and it takes fifteen minutes, because you already decided what to say the last time you spoke to each room.

Then spend fifteen minutes adding new rooms. [Use AI to research venues](use-ai-to-research-venues-for-gigs.html) in a town you have not worked, check what it tells you against the venue's own site, and put the ones that fit into the sheet as Researching.

Then pitch. Two or three [cold pitches](how-to-pitch-a-venue-cold.html) a week is a perfectly good pace, and the email is short enough that the writing is not the hard part.

Finally, ten minutes on the calendar page. Every confirmed date goes up that day, because [a stale calendar quietly costs you gigs](band-gig-calendar-page.html).

Three or four pitches a week is well over a hundred a year. Almost nobody does that, which is exactly why it works.

## The Weekly Hour for Social and Email

The second hour covers everything that talks to people.

Most weeks this is lighter than it sounds, because the heavy lifting happens once a month when you [batch and schedule a month of posts](batch-schedule-band-social-media.html). The weekly hour is for what the queue cannot know in advance.

Check the queue. Post the one live thing that happened this week. If there is no gig, pull something from [the between-gigs list](what-bands-should-post-between-gigs.html) rather than going quiet.

Once a month, the hour becomes the newsletter instead. Twenty minutes with [the monthly template](monthly-band-newsletter-template.html), dates at the top, one story, one ask.

And keep feeding the list. The signup habit lives at the gig, not at the desk, so the desk job is just making sure the welcome email is working and the tags are right.

## Once a Month

Two sittings, neither long.

The first is the planning afternoon: a month of posts built around the gig calendar, scheduled and forgotten about.

The second is fifteen minutes with numbers. [Your website report](monthly-website-report-for-bands.html) tells you which towns keep looking you up, and [your social insights](facebook-instagram-insights-for-bands.html) tell you which posts actually moved people rather than just collecting likes.

One change a month, based on what you saw. Not six. One.

Twice a year, add an hour to read your own site as a stranger would. Is the clip current. Is the lineup right. Does the calendar look like a band that is working. That hour catches more damage than any amount of new content.

## After Every Gig

The smallest block and the one with the best return. Sunday morning, twenty minutes.

Write to the venue. [The thank-you that gets you rebooked](follow-up-after-a-gig-to-get-rebooked.html) goes out within two days, with photos they can post and a specific month attached to the ask.

Write to the other band and the engineer, one line each. [Load-out is where your reputation gets made](musician-networking-at-load-out.html) and Sunday is where it gets confirmed.

If it was a private event, [the week after is worth more than the night was](follow-up-after-wedding-or-private-event.html). Host, planner, coordinator, review link.

Then update the sheet and drop the night's photos into the post bank. Done.

Two hours a week. Twenty minutes after a gig. Two sittings a month. Add it up and it is roughly the time most bands already lose to arguing about a flyer on a Thursday night.

None of it is clever, and none of it requires you to become a marketer. It is the routine I wish somebody had handed me twenty years ago, instead of letting me learn it slowly on somebody else's business and only much later think to point it at my own band.

Start with one block. The Sunday booking hour, probably, since it is the one that puts dates on a calendar. Add the second when the first one feels normal. A year from now the difference will not look like a marketing campaign. It will look like a band that works more than it used to, for reasons nobody outside the band can quite name.
