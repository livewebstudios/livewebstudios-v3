You have a list now. Two hundred people who handed you an email address because they liked a show.

And you have sent them nothing in four months, because every time you sit down to write, it feels like it has to be good.

It does not have to be good. It has to arrive. A band newsletter is dates, one short thing worth reading, and one ask, sent on a schedule people can feel. That is the whole format, and once you have written it twice it takes about twenty minutes.

## Monthly Is Enough

Once a month. Same rough week every month.

Weekly is too much for a band unless you are playing constantly, and people start tuning out. Quarterly is so rare that half the list forgets who you are between sends, which is when the unsubscribes show up.

Monthly is frequent enough that you stay familiar and rare enough that opening it feels like no burden. Pick the week that fits your calendar, the first Tuesday or whatever, and just keep hitting it.

Consistency beats polish here by a mile. A plain, slightly rough email that shows up every month builds something. A beautiful one that shows up whenever you feel inspired does not.

## Dates Go First

Above everything. Before the story, before the photo, before hello.

The single most common reason someone opens a band's email is to find out where you are playing. Make them scroll for it and you have wasted the best thirty seconds you will get from them all month.

Every listing needs the same five things: the date, the venue, the town, the start time, and a link. Three or four upcoming dates is plenty. If the list is long, show the next four and link to your calendar for the rest.

If you have no dates at all, say so in one honest line and move on. "Nothing on the books until March, we're out pitching" is fine. People appreciate a band that does not pretend.

## One Good Story

This is the part that makes it a newsletter instead of a flyer.

One short thing. Four or five sentences. Something that happened, or something you are looking forward to, told the way you would tell a friend.

The room that was dead until a birthday party walked in at ten. The song you have been arguing about adding for a year and finally added. A photo somebody sent you from the second set. A thank-you to the venue that keeps having you back.

What it should not be is a press release about yourselves. Nobody forwards a press release. People forward the thing that made them smile on a train.

Keep it in your own voice. If you would not say it out loud, cut it.

## One Clear Ask

One. Not three.

Every email gets exactly one thing you want the reader to do, and it goes near the bottom where it belongs.

<ul>
  <li>Come to a specific show. Name the date and the room.</li>
  <li>Book us for your party. Link to the contact form.</li>
  <li>Forward this to somebody who would like us.</li>
  <li>Reply and tell us a song you want to hear next time.</li>
</ul>

That last one is quietly the best. Replies are worth more than clicks, because a person who writes back is a person who shows up. And answering them takes five minutes.

Rotate the ask month to month. When every email asks for the same thing, people stop reading the bottom.

## Short Enough to Finish

Here is the template. Copy it and use it forever.

> **Subject:** Two shows in March, and a thank-you
>
> Hey everyone,
>
> **Where we're playing:**
> Fri Mar 6 · [venue], [town] · 9pm · [link]
> Sat Mar 21 · [venue], [town] · 8pm · [link]
>
> **This month:** [four or five sentences. One real thing that happened. A photo if you have a good one.]
>
> **One favor:** [the single ask]
>
> Thanks for reading, and thanks for coming out. It genuinely means a lot.
>
> [Your name]
> [unsubscribe link]

That is it. Dates, story, ask, sign-off.

Read the whole thing on your phone before you send it. If your thumb has to scroll more than twice, cut something. The emails people finish are the emails people open next month.

None of this works without people to send it to, and the easiest place to find them is the room you are already standing in. [Getting signups at the gig](get-email-signups-at-gigs.html) is the front half of this, and [why the list matters at all](why-bands-need-an-email-list.html) is the reason to bother with either.

Twenty minutes, once a month. That is the entire commitment, and it is the closest thing to a guaranteed audience a working band can build.
