Your website has been quietly writing down everything that happens on it. Who showed up, where they came from, what they clicked, what town they were sitting in.

Nobody has ever looked at it.

That is normal, and it is a shame, because a band's website report is one of the few honest signals you get about who actually wants to hire you. Once a month, fifteen minutes, and you will find at least one thing worth acting on. I write these reports for web clients every month, and the surprises in a band's numbers are usually better than the surprises in a business's.

## Your Site Already Knows Things

Two free tools cover everything you need.

Google Analytics tells you what people did once they arrived: how many came, which pages they saw, how long they stayed. Google Search Console tells you what they typed to find you in the first place, which is a different and often more interesting question.

Both are free, both take about twenty minutes to connect, and if somebody built your site there is a good chance one or both is already running. Ask before you set up a second one.

You are not doing analysis here. You are looking at four things and asking whether any of them changed.

## Where Visitors Come From

The first number is how people arrived.

Search means somebody went looking for live music, or for your band by name. Social means a post did its job. Direct means somebody typed your address or had it saved, which usually means a booker or a returning fan. Referral means another site sent them, often a venue's event page.

The shape of that mix tells you what is working. A band getting almost everything from social has an audience that lives on somebody else's platform. A band getting steady search traffic is being found by people who did not already know them, which is where new bookings come from.

Watch it month to month, not day to day. One good night skews a week. A trend takes three months to see.

## Which Pages They Actually Visit

Next, the page list. Sort by most-visited and read the top five.

The two that matter are [your calendar](band-gig-calendar-page.html) and your contact page. Those are the pages people land on when they are deciding something. If your calendar is the second most-viewed page on the site, that is a healthy sign. If nobody ever reaches contact, something between the front door and that page is not doing its job.

Also worth a look: where people arrive. If your homepage is the top entry point, your name is doing the work. If a specific page about weddings or private events is pulling people in from search, that page is earning its keep and probably deserves a second one like it.

And notice what nobody reads. A page with four visits in a month is a page you can stop maintaining.

## Which Towns Keep Looking You Up

This is the fun one, and it is the reason I tell bands to bother.

Analytics shows you roughly where your visitors are. Not who they are, just the town or region. Read that list every month and look for names you do not expect.

Traffic from a town you rarely play is a booking hint sitting in plain sight. Somebody there is looking you up, and people do not look up a band for no reason. Maybe a venue there had a show like yours. Maybe somebody saw you at a private event and went home and searched.

Either way it is a free lead. Pull up the rooms in that town, and go pitch one. That is the whole move, and it works because you are pitching into interest that already exists instead of guessing.

Search Console does the same trick with words. The phrases people typed to find you are sitting right there. If people are finding you by searching for a style of act you barely mention on your site, you have just learned what to put on the homepage.

## One Action a Month

Here is the discipline that keeps this from becoming a hobby.

Look at the four things. Write down one sentence about what you noticed. Then change exactly one thing.

<ul>
  <li>An unexpected town keeps appearing. Pitch two venues there this month.</li>
  <li>Search traffic is flat. Add the towns you play to the pages that never name them.</li>
  <li>People reach the calendar and stop. Put the contact link on the calendar page.</li>
  <li>A search phrase surprises you. Write a page that answers it properly.</li>
</ul>

One change. Then look again next month and see whether it moved.

The temptation is to stare at the numbers and change nothing, or to change six things and learn nothing. Both waste the fifteen minutes. The bands that get something out of this treat it like tuning: small adjustment, listen, adjust again.

If the location data is telling you to show up somewhere, the next step is making sure you turn up in search when someone there goes looking, which is what [local search for bands](local-seo-for-bands.html) is about. The report tells you where the interest is. That part tells you how to be findable once you get there.

Fifteen minutes a month. It is the least glamorous habit in this whole series, and it is the one that most often hands you a gig you would never have thought to chase.
