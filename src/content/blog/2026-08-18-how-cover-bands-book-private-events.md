A bar booker asks one question: can you bring people in?

A couple planning a wedding asks a completely different one. Will you make our night go smoothly, and are you going to embarrass us in front of two hundred guests?

Those are not the same buyer, and a band that talks to both of them the same way loses the second one every time. Private events are where a lot of working cover bands find their steadiest income, and getting into that market is mostly a matter of understanding who is on the other side of the inquiry form.

## A Different Buyer With Different Worries

The bar wants a draw. The private client wants certainty.

Nobody hiring you for a wedding is worried about whether you will fill the room. The room is already full and the guest list was settled months ago. What keeps them up is everything else. Will you turn up. Will you be appropriately dressed. Will you play something their parents can dance to. Will the volume be sane during dinner.

That is the whole difference, and it changes everything about how you present yourself. Draw stops mattering. Reliability and professionalism become the entire pitch.

Corporate is the same buyer in a different suit. An office manager organizing a holiday party is putting their own judgment on the line in front of colleagues. They are buying peace of mind, and they will pay more for it than a bar ever will.

## How Couples and Planners Search

They search for exactly what you would expect, and it is almost never your band's name.

"Wedding band near me." "Live music for corporate event [region]." "Cover band for a party in [town]." They start on a search engine or on a venue's recommended-vendors list, and they compare four or five options in an evening.

Which means being findable for those phrases is most of the game, and it is a different job from being findable to a bar booker. [Local search for bands](local-seo-for-bands.html) covers how that works, and the short version is that you need a page that speaks to this buyer, with the towns and regions you serve written on it in plain sentences.

Venues matter enormously here too. Event spaces keep lists of bands they have worked with and hand them out. Getting onto one of those lists is often just asking the coordinator after a good night, and almost nobody asks.

## What They Need to See Before They Call

Three things, and a band that has all three gets called ahead of a better band that has none.

The first is proof you have done this specific thing. Photos from actual private events. A reception, a party, a corporate room. Bar photos do not reassure a couple, because a packed bar is exactly the atmosphere they are worried about.

The second is a song list. Not a vibe description, an actual list. Private clients want to see names they recognize and to know you can cover a range of ages in one room. Put it on the site, keep it current, and organize it so somebody can skim it in a minute.

The third is a clip of you playing that kind of event. Well dressed, sensible volume, a floor full of people in their good clothes. One clip like that does more than everything else on your website combined for this audience.

## The Inquiry Form

Keep it short, and ask the three things that let you answer intelligently.

<ul>
  <li>The date.</li>
  <li>The town, or the venue if they have one.</li>
  <li>What kind of event it is.</li>
</ul>

Name, email, and a box for anything else. That is the whole form. Every extra field costs you inquiries, and the details get sorted out in the conversation anyway.

Then answer fast. Same day if you possibly can. Private clients are usually contacting several bands in one sitting, and the first thoughtful reply has an advantage that is hard to overstate.

They will ask what you cost, sometimes in the very first message. Have an answer ready before you need one. What that number is depends on your market, your size, and how long you play, and it is a conversation for your band and not for a blog post. Just do not be caught improvising it.

## Reviews From the Last Event

Here is the compounding part.

A couple deciding between two bands they have never seen will read reviews, and in this market reviews do more work than anything else you own. One band with nine detailed reviews from real weddings and one band with none is not a close comparison.

So ask, every single time, in [the week after the event](follow-up-after-wedding-or-private-event.html) while everybody is still glowing. The host, the planner, and the venue coordinator. Three asks, and you will usually get one.

Send a direct link so it takes a single tap. Anything more than that and it quietly goes on a list of things people meant to do.

Planners and coordinators deserve particular attention, because they are not one booking. A good coordinator books dozens of events a year and recommends the same handful of bands over and over. Being on that short list is worth more than any pitch email you will ever write, and it is earned entirely by being easy to work with on the night.

That is the whole market, really. Show up early, dress the part, keep the volume reasonable during dinner, and be pleasant to everybody in a headset. Do that consistently and the work starts arriving without you chasing it.
