There are forty-one videos on your channel. Full sets, single songs, a rehearsal from 2022, somebody's phone footage from a parking lot.

A booker will watch about eight seconds of one of them, if they watch anything at all.

Which means the only real question is which eight seconds they land on, and right now that is being decided by an upload date instead of by you. Picking one clip and putting it where people cannot miss it is a twenty-minute job that outperforms almost anything else on your website.

## Bookers Watch Seconds, Not Songs

Nobody is sitting through your set.

They are skimming, on a phone, between two other things, and they are answering a single question: does this band work in a room? Eight seconds is genuinely enough to answer it, and they will not give you more until you have earned it.

That changes what a good clip is. It is not the performance you are proudest of. It is the eight seconds that most obviously say yes.

So the clip has to open strong. Not with a wide shot of an empty stage or a count-off. Start where the song is already going, ideally somewhere a room is visibly reacting.

## The Crowd Is the Star

This is the part bands get backwards most often.

Every instinct says the video should be about the band. Frame everybody, get the vocals clean, make sure the camera is on whoever is taking the lead.

But the buyer is not evaluating the band in isolation. They are evaluating what happens to a room when the band plays. So the most persuasive footage you own is almost always the footage where you can see people dancing, singing, holding phones up, or just standing closer to the stage than they were ten minutes ago.

A slightly rough clip with a visible crowd beats a pristine clip of five people playing to an empty floor. Every time, for every kind of buyer.

Look through your footage with that filter alone and the choice usually makes itself.

## Picking the One

Sit down with the band and actually do this. It takes half an hour.

<ul>
  <li>Pull every clip from the last eighteen months. Ignore anything older.</li>
  <li>Drop anything where the room is empty or the lineup has changed.</li>
  <li>Of what is left, keep the three with the most visible crowd reaction.</li>
  <li>Watch the first eight seconds of each with the sound down. Pick the one that still works.</li>
  <li>Trim the front so it starts on the strongest moment.</li>
</ul>

That sound-down step feels odd and it is the most useful one, because it isolates exactly what a booker is unconsciously reading: body language, the room, whether this looks like a good night.

Length: under two minutes for a featured clip, and you are hoping for the first eight seconds to do the work anyway. If you have a longer full-song video, keep it further down the page for the people who want more.

One more thing. If a member left, retire the clip. A booker who sees a lineup that no longer exists starts wondering what else on the site is out of date.

## Where It Goes on Your Site

Above the fold on the homepage. That phrase means visible without scrolling, on a phone, and it is where the clip belongs.

Not on a video page. Not three clicks in. Not behind a "media" link. The single strongest thing you own should be the first thing a stranger encounters, and everything else can live deeper.

The same clip goes at the top of your press kit, for the same reason. [Leading with the draw](what-belongs-in-a-band-epk.html) means the clip and the room list come before anything about the band's history.

Make sure it plays without asking anybody to sign in anywhere. An embed that demands an account is a clip that does not get watched, and a link that opens a different app loses half the people who tap it.

## Retire the Clips That Don't Sell You

The last step is deleting things, which is harder than it sounds.

Every weak clip on your channel is competing with your strong one. Somebody searching your band's name might land on a two-year-old phone recording from the back of a room with terrible audio, and that becomes their impression.

So prune. Unlist the ones that do not help. Keep the archive if it means something to you, just do not leave it in the path of somebody deciding whether to hire you.

Then set a reminder to redo this once a year. New footage arrives constantly, and the clip that was your best in January is often not your best by autumn. Twenty minutes annually keeps the front door of your band looking like the band you are now.

A booker gives you thirty seconds and spends eight of them on video. [That whole first impression](what-venue-bookers-look-at-first.html) is decided in a window you do not get to be present for, so the one thing you can control is making sure the strongest eight seconds you own are the ones sitting in front of them.
