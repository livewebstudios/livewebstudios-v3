You post the flyer for Saturday. Your followers see it. Some of them were coming anyway.

The venue has four times your following. The other band on the bill has twice. Neither of them knows the post exists, because nobody tagged anybody.

That is a few thousand people who might have seen your show announcement and did not, for free, because of a step that takes six seconds. Tagging is the least glamorous thing in this whole series and it is probably the highest return.

## Reach You Don't Have to Build

Building an audience is slow. Borrowing one is instant.

When you tag a venue, the post can surface to people who follow that venue. Those people already go out to see live music in your area. That is a better audience than a general one, and it took you one tap to reach it.

Same with the other act on the bill. Their followers are people who go to shows at rooms like this one. Half of them have never heard of you, and now they have, in a context that says you are worth an evening.

The venue also sees it, which matters for reasons we will get to.

## Tagging Done Right

There is a wrong way, so let us be specific.

<ul>
  <li>Tag the venue's actual account, not a location pin with a similar name. Check that you have the real one, because most rooms have two or three stray listings.</li>
  <li>Tag the account, not just the text. Typing the venue's name is not a tag. It has to be the linked handle.</li>
  <li>Tag the other bands by their band accounts, and tag the individual players if you know them.</li>
  <li>Add the location tag as well as the account tag. They do different jobs.</li>
  <li>Do it on every post about the show, not just the first one.</li>
</ul>

One thing to avoid: tagging accounts that have nothing to do with the show. A local paper, a brand you like, a national act. It looks like you are fishing, people notice, and it makes the real tags read as noise.

If you already run [the four-post pattern around every gig](four-post-pattern-to-promote-a-gig.html), the tags belong in all four of those posts. Announcement, reminder, day-of, thank-you. Four chances at the venue's audience instead of one.

## Share Their Posts Too

This is where most bands stop, and it is where the actual relationship starts.

When the venue posts about your show, share it. When the other band posts their flyer, share that. Put it on your story, add a line, send people over.

It costs you nothing and it changes how you are seen by everybody involved. The other band notices. The venue definitely notices. Six months later when that band needs an opener, or when the venue is filling a slot, you are the one they think of. Not because you asked. Because you were useful.

Same goes for the room itself. The venue posts about a new menu or a different band's sold-out night, and you share it occasionally. You are a regular at that place now, in public, and regulars get treated like regulars.

## Instagram Collab Posts

Worth knowing about specifically, because it is the strongest version of all this.

A collab post appears on both accounts' profiles as the same post, with both names on it. Not a share, not a tag. One post living in two places, collecting likes and comments from both audiences in the same thread.

Propose it to the other band a week out. One of you makes the post, invites the other as a collaborator, and they accept. Two minutes of coordination.

Ask the venue too. Some rooms will say yes and some have a policy against it, so ask once and do not push.

The only real trick is asking early. Nobody wants to coordinate a post at four in the afternoon on show day, which is exactly when most bands remember to think about it. <!-- VERIFY -->

## What Venues Remember

Here is the part that turns this from a social media habit into a booking strategy.

A booker is deciding between two bands who both drew a decent crowd last time. One of them promoted the show and tagged the room in every post. The other one played well and posted nothing.

The room gets rebooked by the first band. Every time. Not because the booker is scoring your social media, but because the whole night was easier and the venue got something out of it beyond a bar tab.

Venues track who helps them fill the room. Some do it on a spreadsheet and most do it in their head, but they all do it. Being the band that promotes is one of the cheapest reputations to earn in this business, and it compounds. Promote the other acts and the room, consistently, and within a year you are on the short list for the good nights.

Being generous with other bands is not a strategy at first. It just turns into one. The act you shared last spring is the act that recommends you for a festival slot next summer, and that happens often enough that I have stopped thinking of it as luck. And when you are the one posting clips [from the stage](instagram-reels-for-bands.html), tag the room in those too. Same six seconds, same compounding.
