You post the show to four thousand followers. A few dozen see it.

Nothing went wrong. That's just the arrangement. You built an audience on a platform, and the platform decides who hears from you and when.

An email list is the one audience nobody gets to ration. Somebody hands you their address, you write, it arrives. That's the whole pitch, and it's why a list of two hundred real fans can put more people in a room than a follower count ten times its size.

## You Don't Own Your Followers

Say it plainly. Your followers are not yours. They're the platform's users, and you get access to them on terms that change without warning.

I'm not being dramatic about this. I watched a client's restaurant lose most of its reach in a single year, with the same posts and the same customers. Nothing they did caused it. Nothing they did could fix it, either.

The people on your email list are different. They gave you an address on purpose. Until they unsubscribe, you can reach every one of them.

## What Email Does That Social Can't

It lands. That's the first thing. No feed, no ranking, no guessing whether tonight is the night the algorithm likes you.

It also arrives at full length. You can put four dates, a photo, and a story in one email without anyone scrolling past it. Try fitting that in a caption.

And it's personal in a way a public post isn't. Somebody reading your email is alone with it. They're likelier to actually read the thing than to half-watch it go by between a news headline and somebody's dog.

There's a quieter benefit too. An email list travels with you. Bands break up and reform. Projects change names. The list is yours through all of it, which is the same argument for owning a real website instead of [renting a Facebook page](why-a-facebook-page-isnt-a-band-website.html).

## It Doesn't Need to Be Big

This is where most musicians talk themselves out of starting. Two hundred addresses doesn't feel like a list. It feels like a rounding error next to a follower count.

Flip it around. Two hundred people who asked to hear from you, most of whom open the email, in towns where you actually play. That's a real room's worth of people, and you can reach all of them on a Tuesday.

Small and real beats big and rented. Every time.

The other thing a small list has going for it is that it grows every night you play. Twenty new addresses a month is a couple of people per gig, and in a year that's a list worth having.

## Picking a Tool

Don't agonize here. Mailchimp is the one most people land on and it's fine. So are the others. Pick one with a free tier, make an account, and move on.

Two things actually matter when you choose:

- You can embed a signup form on your own website.
- You can get a signup link you can turn into a QR code.

That's it. Everything else is a feature you will not use in your first year. The tool is not the hard part. Remembering to send is the hard part.

Put the signup form on your own site rather than only on the tool's hosted page. The address people type should be yours.

## Your First Send

Write it this week. Not next month, this week, while the account is still open in a tab.

Keep it short:

> Subject: Two dates coming up, and a new song
>
> Hi, it's [NAME] from [BAND].
>
> Thanks for signing up. Two things:
>
> We're at [VENUE] in [TOWN] on [DATE], [TIME]. And we're at [VENUE] on [DATE].
>
> We pulled [SONG] back into the set for the first time in years. Here's a clip from rehearsal: [LINK]
>
> Hope to see you out there.
>
> [NAME]

That's a complete first email. Dates up top, one thing worth reading, a link to the site. Nobody is expecting a newsletter that looks like a magazine.

Then send again next month. Consistency is what turns a list into an audience, and the only way to be consistent is to keep it small enough that sending never feels like a project.

Start collecting addresses at the next gig. In a year you'll wish you'd started tonight.
