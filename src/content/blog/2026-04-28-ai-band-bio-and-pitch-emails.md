You ask an AI for a band bio. Eight seconds later you have three paragraphs, and every one of them could be about any band that has ever existed.

Delete it. Then do it again properly, because the tool is genuinely useful. It is just useless at guessing.

A blank page is the expensive part of writing, and AI removes the blank page. What it cannot do is know anything true about your band. Give it real material and a sense of how you talk, and the second draft is something you can actually send.

## Why AI Bios All Sound the Same

An AI writes from the average of everything it has read. Ask it about a band and it reaches for the words that show up in ten thousand other band bios.

So you get a paragraph about energy and connection and a show people will remember. Technically English. Says nothing.

The problem is not the tool. The problem is the prompt. "Write a bio for my band" contains no information, so the model fills the gap with the most generic thing available. Garbage in, beige out.

I run into this constantly in the web business. The first draft of anything is generic until somebody feeds it something only they could know.

## Feed It Real Details

Before you open the chat window, write down the facts. Ten minutes, in a notes app, no editing.

<ul>
  <li>Rooms you have played, by name, and roughly how often.</li>
  <li>What happens at your show. What the room does during the third song.</li>
  <li>Who hires you: bars, private events, festivals, a mix.</li>
  <li>Size of the act, instrumentation as roles, how long the sets run.</li>
  <li>Anything true and specific. Two of you have played together since high school. You have a horn section. You do a four-hour night with no repeats.</li>
</ul>

Paste that whole mess into the prompt and tell it what you want:

> Here are the real facts about my band. Write a 120-word bio for a venue booker, in plain language. No adjectives about energy. Lead with the rooms we have played and who hires us. Do not invent anything that is not in my notes.

That last sentence matters more than it looks. AI will happily fill a gap with something plausible, and a plausible lie in your bio is a problem you find out about in front of a booker.

Same method works for a pitch email, with one change: tell it the specific room and the specific night you are pitching. A pitch that could be sent to anybody reads like it was.

## Give It Your Voice

Here is the step that turns a usable draft into one that sounds like a person.

Keep a short note with samples of how you actually write. A few texts you sent about a gig. A paragraph from your last newsletter. Something you posted after a good night. Two hundred words is plenty.

Paste it in and say: match this voice. Same rhythm, same vocabulary, same level of formality.

I keep a file like that for my own writing and paste it into every draft. It is not a trick. It is just the fastest way to tell a machine what you sound like, and it works better than any amount of describing yourself as friendly and down to earth.

Then tell it what to avoid. Give it your own kill list, which brings us to the fun part.

## Words to Delete on Sight

Every AI draft arrives carrying the same suitcase. Open the draft, search for these, and cut them before you read anything else.

<!-- banned-by-design: this is the delete list the brief calls for -->
<ul>
  <li>musical journey</li>
  <li>passionate about music</li>
  <li>unforgettable experience</li>
  <li>bring the house down</li>
  <li>high-energy</li>
  <li>take your band to the next level</li>
  <li>dynamic, electrifying, captivating, and anything else that is a compliment you are paying yourself</li>
</ul>
<!-- /banned-by-design -->

Notice what they have in common. Every one is a claim about how good you are, made by you, with no evidence attached. A booker discounts all of it automatically.

Replace each one with a fact. "High energy" becomes "the dance floor fills by the second song." "Unforgettable" becomes the name of the room and how many times they have had you back. Facts survive skepticism. Adjectives do not.

This is exactly what belongs at the top of a press kit too, and [the EPK section on leading with your draw](what-belongs-in-a-band-epk.html) is the same idea aimed at a different page.

## Read It Out Loud

Last step, and do not skip it.

Read the final draft out loud, standing up, like you are telling a friend about your band. Every sentence you stumble on is a sentence you would never say. Fix those and you are done.

It takes two minutes and it catches everything the previous four steps missed. A bio that survives being read aloud sounds like a person wrote it, because at that point one did.

The pattern here is the same one behind [using AI for venue research](use-ai-to-research-venues-for-gigs.html). The tool is fast at the tedious part and confidently wrong about the specific part, so you supply the specifics and you check the output. Draft in seconds, five minutes of your own judgment on top, and what goes out the door is yours.
