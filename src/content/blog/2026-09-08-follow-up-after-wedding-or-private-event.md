The dance floor did not empty once after nine. People you have never met told you it was the best wedding band they had seen.

Four of those guests are getting married in the next two years.

None of them will remember your name by Thursday. That is not a failure of the night, it is just how memory works at a wedding, and it is why the week afterward is worth more to your calendar than the gig itself was. A handful of short messages, sent while everybody is still glowing, turn one good night into the next three bookings.

## The Best Lead Is the Last Party

A private event is a room full of people who just watched you do the exact job they might need doing.

Compare that to any other lead source you have. A cold pitch reaches somebody with no evidence. A social post reaches somebody who has never seen you play. A wedding guest watched two hundred people have a good time because of you, for four hours, at close range.

That is the warmest audience you will ever stand in front of, and almost every band walks away from it without a plan.

The window is about a week. After that the couple is on a honeymoon, the planner is on to the next event, and the whole thing recedes into a pleasant blur.

## Thank the Host

Start here, two or three days after. Email, not text, because they are going to forward it.

> Hi [name],
>
> Thank you for having us on Saturday. Genuinely one of our favorite nights this year, and your friends can dance.
>
> We grabbed a few photos from the reception, attached. Use them however you like.
>
> If anyone asks who the band was, we'd be glad to hear from them. And congratulations again, from all of us.
>
> [Your name]

Short, warm, no invoice talk, no hard sell. The referral ask is one line and it is phrased as a favor they might do rather than a favor you need.

The photos matter more than the words. They arrive before the professional photographer's gallery does, which means for about two weeks your pictures are the only pictures anybody has seen. They get posted, shared, and shown around, and your name travels with them.

## Thank the Planner and the Venue Coordinator

Separate message, same week, and this one is the business relationship.

The couple hires a band once. A planner hires bands twenty times a year, and a venue coordinator gets asked "who would you recommend?" constantly. These two people are worth more to your calendar over five years than any single client.

Keep it brief and professional.

> Hi [name],
>
> Thanks for making Saturday easy on our end. The timeline ran smoothly and your team was great about the load-in.
>
> We'd love to be on your list for anything similar. Our dates and a clip from a recent reception are here: [link]
>
> Thanks again,
> [Your name]

Notice there is no ask for a referral in that one. You are asking to be on a list, which is a smaller and more answerable request, and being on the list is how the referrals come.

Do this after every private event, even the ones that were only fine. Coordinators remember the bands who wrote.

## Ask for a Review While It's Fresh

Same week, and make it a single tap.

Send the direct link. Not "leave us a review," not the name of the platform, the actual link that opens the review box. Every extra step loses people who genuinely meant to do it.

Ask the host, the planner, and the coordinator. Three asks per event, and you will usually get one, which over a year of private work builds something that quietly sells for you while you sleep.

What to ask for: a sentence about the event and what the floor was like. Specific reviews persuade the next couple far more than "great band."

It is the same habit that makes you findable to the next couple who goes looking, and it is the single cheapest thing you can do for [the private-event side of the business](how-cover-bands-book-private-events.html).

## Make Referrals Easy

Last piece, and it is one sentence and one link.

People want to recommend a band they liked. They just need something to send. So give them the thing.

> If a friend is looking for a band, this is the page to send them: [link]

That link goes to the page on your site built for private clients, with the song list, the event photos, and a short inquiry form. Not your homepage, not your Facebook page. The page that answers the questions a couple asks.

Add the guests to your list too, if you collected any at the event. Tag them so they get the occasional show announcement, and keep the planners and coordinators on the professional side of the list, where a short availability note a few times a year belongs.

Then log all of it. Who you contacted, what they said, when to check back. The same [follow-up discipline that gets you rebooked at a bar](follow-up-after-a-gig-to-get-rebooked.html) works on private clients, with a longer clock: a planner you wrote to in June is worth a short note in January.

Three emails and a review link. Half an hour, once, in the week after a night that already went well.
