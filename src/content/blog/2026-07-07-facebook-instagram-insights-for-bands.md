The post with the moody black-and-white shot got ninety likes. Everyone in the band was thrilled.

Eleven people came to the show.

The post nobody mentioned, the ugly one with the flyer and the venue tagged, got thirty-one likes and forty-two link clicks. That one filled a corner of the room. Likes and results are two different scoreboards, and almost every band is watching the wrong one.

## Likes Aren't the Scoreboard

A like costs somebody half a second. It means "I saw this and did not dislike it."

It does not mean they noted the date. It does not mean they clicked. It definitely does not mean they showed up.

Worse, likes mostly come from people who already follow you, which means a heavily liked post can be a post that reached nobody new. A band optimizing for likes ends up making posts for the two hundred people who were coming anyway.

None of which makes likes useless. They are just the least informative number on the page, and they happen to be the one displayed biggest.

## The Numbers That Matter

Four, and they all mean something concrete.

<ul>
  <li><strong>Reach.</strong> How many separate people saw it. This is your audience size for that post, and it is the only number that tells you whether you got outside your own circle.</li>
  <li><strong>Link clicks.</strong> Somebody wanted more. For a gig post this is the closest thing to intent you will ever measure.</li>
  <li><strong>Shares.</strong> Someone put their own name behind it. One share from a local is worth more than forty likes from anywhere.</li>
  <li><strong>Saves.</strong> Somebody filed it to come back to. On a show announcement that often means "I am thinking about going."</li>
</ul>

Shares and saves are the two most people never look at, and they are the two that predict a room.

Follower growth is worth a glance once a month, and no more than that. It moves slowly and it does not tell you what to do differently on Tuesday.

## Where to Find Them

On Instagram, open a post and tap the small link under it that shows the stats. For a whole-account view, look in the professional dashboard on your profile. <!-- VERIFY -->

On Facebook, the numbers live in the page's insights area, reachable from your page tools. <!-- VERIFY -->

Both companies rename and move these screens constantly, so treat any specific menu path as a rough guide rather than instructions. What you are looking for is consistent even when the labels change: a per-post breakdown, and a list you can sort.

Your account has to be set up as a professional or business account to see any of it. If you are running the band off a personal profile, that is the first thing to change, and it takes about two minutes.

## Compare Posts, Not Days

Here is the mistake that makes this whole exercise useless.

People open insights, see that last Tuesday was worse than the Tuesday before, and conclude the algorithm is punishing them. That comparison means nothing. Different post, different time, different content, different week.

Compare like with like. All your gig announcements against each other. All your day-of posts against each other. All your clips against each other.

Now you learn something real. Announcements with the flyer image do better than announcements with a band photo. Clips under fifteen seconds hold people longer than clips over thirty. Posts that went up at five in the afternoon beat posts that went up at nine in the morning.

Three or four comparisons like that, and you have a genuine house style based on evidence rather than on whichever bandmate is loudest.

## Connect It to Real Results

Last step, and the only one that matters in the end.

Social numbers are a proxy. The real scoreboard is people in the room, names on the email list, and inquiries in the inbox. So tie them together, roughly, once a month.

Write down three things: how many link clicks the gig posts got, how many new email signups you took that month, and how many booking inquiries came in. Put them next to the number of people who showed up.

Do that for six months and patterns appear that no individual insights screen will ever show you. Maybe shares predict attendance better than anything else. Maybe the clips do nothing for turnout but everything for signups. That is your band's own data, about your own town, and it beats general advice every time.

This is the same fifteen-minute habit as [reading your website report](monthly-website-report-for-bands.html), and it works best done in the same sitting. One page of numbers, one thing you change, then back to playing. And the pattern you are testing against is [the four posts around every show](four-post-pattern-to-promote-a-gig.html), which is exactly the kind of repeated structure that makes comparison possible.
