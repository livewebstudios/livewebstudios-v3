A booker at a busy room gets more band emails in a week than anyone could ever listen to. So they don't listen. Not at first.

They click and they skim. They make a call in about thirty seconds, and most bands never find out they lost the gig inside that window.

I get to watch this from two angles. I play keyboards in working bands, and I build websites for a living. What bookers check first isn't a mystery. It's pretty consistent, and almost all of it is fixable in a weekend.

## They Check If You're Real

Before anyone hears a note, a booker wants to know one thing. Is this an actual working act, or a few friends and a rehearsal space?

That judgment happens fast. They look for a real website with your band's name in the address. Then they look at the calendar. If the last date listed was fourteen months ago, you just told them you're not working, even if you played last Saturday.

A Facebook page alone doesn't clear this bar anymore. Neither does a link page with six buttons on it. Bookers want one address that shows somebody takes this band seriously.

## They Watch a Few Seconds of Video

Not the whole song. A few seconds, maybe a little more if you've earned it.

What they're watching for is simple. Does this band hold a room? A crowd dancing and singing along does more for you than a perfect mix ever will.

The mistake I see constantly is burying the best clip. It's three pages deep on a video channel, or it's a full-set upload nobody is going to scrub through. Put your single strongest live clip where a booker sees it without scrolling. One great clip, front and center.

## They Look for Proof Someone Else Took a Chance on You

Bookers are cautious for a good reason. A bad night costs them money at the bar and a little trust with their regulars.

So they look for signs other rooms already trusted you. A short list of venues you've played helps, and so does a line from a venue manager who'd have you back. Even a photo with the venue's sign in the frame counts.

You don't need a long list. You need a real one. A handful of rooms a booker recognizes beats a long list of places they've never heard of.

## They Find Out How Hard You Are to Work With

This one surprises people. A booker is quietly asking whether you'll be a headache.

Can they find a way to contact you in one click? Is there a stage plot and a rider, or will the sound engineer be guessing at seven o'clock? Does your bio take one minute to read, or ten?

Clear, current information tells a booker you're organized. Organized bands show up on time and don't argue about the backline. That's worth more to a venue than most bands realize.

## The Thirty-Second Test

Pull up your own website on your phone. Pretend you've never heard of your band, and give it thirty seconds.

- Is your band's name in the web address?
- Are there upcoming dates on the calendar?
- Can you see a live clip without scrolling?
- Can you find a way to book the band in one tap?
- Is there a stage plot or rider a sound engineer could actually use?

Any answer that's a no is your weekend project.

## Most of This Isn't Talent

Here's the part I'm most grateful I learned. Plenty of great bands lose gigs to decent bands with better presentation. That isn't fair. It's just how it goes when a booker has thirty seconds and a full inbox.

The good news is that presentation can be learned. I picked most of it up building websites for other people's businesses, long before I thought to use it for my own gigs. It works the same way for a five-piece cover band as it does for a plumber. People want proof you're good, and proof you're easy to hire.

Give them both in the first thirty seconds.
