The big moving image at the top of a homepage used to be a budget line only larger companies could justify. A crew. A location. A shoot day, then an edit, then a revision round. Now we make those films from a paragraph of text, and the ones on our own site were made exactly that way.

Here's how it works, what it's good for, and where it still falls down.

## What a hero film actually is

A hero film is the short looping video behind the headline on a website. Eight to fifteen seconds, usually. No sound. It sets a mood before anyone reads a word.

It doesn't need to tell a story. It needs to feel right. A slow push across a workshop bench. Steam off a pan in a dim kitchen. Light moving across water. The job is atmosphere, and atmosphere happens to be the thing text-to-video tools are best at.

## How we make them

We use Higgsfield for most of our film work. The process is closer to directing than to shooting.

### Write the shot, not the idea

"A video about our bakery" gets you something generic. A usable prompt reads like a shot list: what's in frame, where the camera sits, how it moves, what the light is doing, what lens it would have been shot on. Specific beats clever every time.

### Generate a lot, keep a little

We run many variations of every shot. Most get thrown out. A hand has six fingers. A shadow moves the wrong way. The motion is too fast to loop cleanly. Picking the one clip that holds up is a human job, and it's most of the job.

### Cut it for the web

The keeper gets trimmed to a clean loop, color-matched to the site, and compressed hard so it doesn't slow the page. A beautiful film that makes the site load slowly is a bad trade. A still image version ships with it for phones and slow connections.

AI generates the footage. A human principal picks the takes, directs the revisions, and decides when it's done. Nothing goes on a site because a model produced it. It goes on because someone looked at it and said yes.

## Where it works

Generated film is strong for anything that sells a feeling more than a fact.

- **Mood and texture.** Materials, light, weather, motion. Restaurants, spas, event venues, and trades that want to look sharp.
- **Abstract backgrounds.** Shapes, glows, slow geometric motion. Our own site runs on these, including the hologram backgrounds behind several section heroes.
- **Concepts that would be expensive to shoot.** A drone pass over a place that doesn't need to be a specific place. A night scene without a night crew.

The same toolkit covers more than hero loops. We've done animation, 3D character work, and hundreds of photo manipulations and composites. You can see a range of that on our [proof page](../proof.html).

## Where it doesn't

This is the part most AI video pitches skip.

**It can't show your real stuff.** Generated film invents. If a customer needs to see your actual storefront, your actual crew, or the actual deck you built, you need a camera. Passing off generated footage as your real work is a lie, and customers who notice will not trust you again.

**People are still risky.** Faces and hands are where generated video goes wrong most often. We keep people out of frame or at a distance in most hero work, and every prompt we write says no faces unless there's a very good reason.

**Text and logos come out garbled.** Generated signs, labels, and logos rarely hold up. We add real text and branding in the site itself, never inside the film.

**Specific motion is hard to control.** "The camera pushes in slowly" works. "The chef flips the pancake at second four and it lands perfectly" usually doesn't.

For photos of real people and real products, a different set of rules applies. We covered that in [AI Photo Work: Headshots, Products, and the Truth About Editing](ai-photo-work-the-truth.html).

## Is it honest?

We think it is, as long as you're clear about what the film is doing. A mood loop behind a headline is set dressing, the same as a stock video would have been five years ago. Nobody believes the stock-footage coffee cup was poured in your café. The line gets crossed when generated media claims to be evidence: your team, your jobs, your results. We don't make that kind.

## What you need to bring

Less than a shoot, but not nothing.

- **A clear sense of the feeling.** Warm or cool. Busy or calm. Industrial or soft. Two or three reference images help more than a long brief.
- **Your brand colors.** So the film lands inside the design instead of fighting it.
- **Real photos for the parts that must be real.** The generated film sets the mood. Your own photos prove the work.

## What it costs you in time

A shoot takes a day of your business plus a week or two of edits. A generated hero film takes a conversation, then a round of review when we show you the options. You look at a few takes and pick one, or tell us what's off. That's the whole ask.

## Should your site have one?

Not always. A plumber's site is usually better served by a fast page, a phone number up top, and one good photo of a real truck. A film won't win that customer. Speed and trust will.

But for businesses that sell an experience, a place, or a feeling, a good hero film changes how the first five seconds on your site feel. And it no longer takes a production budget to get one.
